# Voice and content

## 1. Communication goal

Write like a collaborative maker: someone approachable who shares ideas freely, cares deeply about craft, follows curiosity, and is willing to make, test, break, and improve the work.

The product should make users feel capable and included. It should respect the labor behind good work and help people move from an idea to something real.

## 2. Messaging foundation

Use these beliefs to decide what a message should emphasize:

1. **Design belongs to everyone.** Present design as a way of thinking and making, not as a protected job title.
2. **Craft creates the difference.** Celebrate purpose, pride, precision, and care for details.
3. **An idea is only the start.** Emphasize making, building, sharing, iterating, and closing the gap between imagination and reality.
4. **Collaboration enables better products.** Use this as supporting context, not as an empty slogan.

For Figma-specific messaging, the source vision is to make design accessible to all and the mission is to help teams collaborate visually. For unrelated apps, use the broader principles without copying corporate claims or implying affiliation.

## 3. Voice traits

The voice stays consistent; tone changes with context.

| Trait | What it means | Use | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conversational | We listen and invite people in | Contractions, everyday words, second person, questions when useful | Fluff, forced enthusiasm, pandering |
| Down-to-earth | We understand real work | Clear benefits, honest limits, real examples, humble confidence | Boasting, vague superlatives, overpromising |
| Clever | We find a fresh angle | Light wordplay, rhythm, contrast, a familiar phrase with a relevant twist | Confusing jokes, strained alliteration, cleverness in serious moments |
| Nerdy | We enjoy the details | Specific feature language, familiar maker terms, a clear explanation of why | Unexplained jargon, academic stiffness, trivia with no user value |

Apply the traits over time; every string does not need to express all four.

## 4. Tone by situation

### Clear and empathetic

Use for errors, permissions, billing, support, settings, documentation, destructive actions, and task completion.

- State what happened.
- Explain the consequence.
- Offer the next useful action.
- Remove jokes, blame, and unnecessary excitement.

Example:

> We couldn't publish this version. Your edits are saved—check your connection and try again.

### Encouraging and lightly cheeky

Use for onboarding, empty states, feature discovery, progress, email, and social content.

- Encourage action without manufacturing urgency.
- Use one small moment of wit at most.
- Keep the task and benefit obvious.

Example:

> Nothing here yet. Add your first teammate and put a few more cursors to work.

### Aspirational and imaginative

Use for a product introduction, launch, campaign, keynote, or high-level landing page.

- Lead with possibility, then prove it with a concrete capability.
- Keep claims achievable.
- Connect imagination to making, building, or shipping.

Example:

> Start with a rough idea. Shape it into something your team can build.

## 5. Core writing rules

- Use sentence case for all UI and marketing copy.
- Prefer active voice and direct verbs.
- Use contractions: “don't,” “can't,” and “we're.”
- Address the reader as “you” when it makes the instruction clearer.
- Use short paragraphs and common words.
- Lead with the user's task or benefit, not the company.
- Explain what a feature does with specific nouns and verbs.
- Keep one idea per sentence and one job per message.
- Use exclamation marks, semicolons, and parentheses sparingly.
- Write lines people can picture. Concrete work beats abstract benefit language.
- Be proud of the product without bragging.
- Match the audience's technical depth, then translate unfamiliar terms.
- Reread for inclusion, brand belief alignment, and unnecessary words.
- Follow the project's editorial style guide; where none exists, use AP style for capitalization and punctuation.

## 6. UI content patterns

### Buttons and actions

- Start with a specific verb: “Create file,” “Invite teammate,” “Publish changes,” “Try again.”
- Match the label to the result. Avoid generic “Submit,” “OK,” or “Yes” when a clearer action fits.
- Keep destructive actions explicit: “Delete project,” not “Continue.”
- Do not add punctuation to button labels.

### Navigation and labels

- Use familiar nouns: “Files,” “Projects,” “Settings,” “Members.”
- Keep parallel items grammatically consistent.
- Avoid invented names when a common category already exists.
- Use Figma Mono for a visible eyebrow or metadata label only when it adds real hierarchy.

### Forms

- Label fields persistently; placeholders are examples, not labels.
- Put requirements and format guidance before an error occurs.
- Explain errors next to the affected field and preserve the user's input.
- Use plain recovery language: “Enter a work email,” not “Invalid input.”

### Empty states

- Explain why the area is empty when it may be unclear.
- Offer one primary next step.
- A small clever line is fine if it does not hide the action.
- Use a spot illustration only when it supports the message and does not overpower it.

### Success and confirmation

- Confirm the completed action and important consequence.
- Keep routine confirmations brief: “Link copied” or “Changes published.”
- Reserve celebration for meaningful milestones. Do not add an exclamation mark to every success state.

### Errors

Use this structure:

1. What happened: “We couldn't upload the file.”
2. What remains true: “Your project is still saved.”
3. What to do: “Choose a file under 25 MB and try again.”

Never blame the user, make a joke at their expense, or replace useful information with “Something went wrong.”

### Destructive and irreversible actions

- Name the affected object and outcome.
- State whether recovery is possible.
- Use a specific confirmation label.
- Avoid cleverness and urgency.

Example:

> Delete “Launch plan”? This removes the project for everyone and can't be undone.

### Tooltips and help

- Explain the control's effect, not just its visible label.
- Put essential guidance in the interface rather than hiding it in a tooltip.
- Link to deeper help only after giving a useful summary.

## 7. AI language

Treat AI as a capability that supports human craft and judgment.

### Do

- Name the actual operation: summarize, sort, rename, draft, generate, find, or connect.
- State what the user can review, change, or undo.
- Describe the time or effort saved without diminishing the work.
- Be transparent about uncertainty, limitations, data use, and irreversible effects.
- Keep the user as the actor: “Draft a summary” is better than “Let AI do your work.”

### Don't

- Say the product replaces designers, developers, writers, or the user's judgment.
- Claim work happens perfectly or entirely by itself.
- Use “magic,” “revolutionary,” “effortless,” or similar language instead of explaining the function.
- Hide a consequential AI action behind a vague label.
- Promise accuracy the system cannot guarantee.

Example:

| Avoid | Prefer |
|---|---|
| Let AI build your prototype | Connect prototype flows automatically |
| Your work, done magically | Draft the first pass, then make it yours |
| Instant perfect summaries | Create a summary you can review and edit |

## 8. Vocabulary

### Useful words

Use naturally when they are specific to the message:

- co-create
- together
- make
- big ideas
- collaborate
- build
- flow

### Words to question

These are not absolute bans, but they often create the wrong tone.

| Question this | Why | Usually try |
|---|---|---|
| dream | Too lofty | idea, goal, plan |
| creativity | Too broad without context | design, write, make, explore |
| playful | Ambiguous | light, lively, cheeky, relaxed |
| supercharge | Marketing language | speed up, simplify, help |
| magical | Diminishes craft or hides function | name the concrete behavior |
| artist | Too poetic or exclusive in general product copy | maker, designer, creator, teammate |
| utilize | Stiff | use |
| seamless | Often unprovable | explain what is connected or removed |
| revolutionary | Boastful and vague | name the measurable change |
| easy / simple | Can dismiss real difficulty | describe the reduced step or clearer path |

## 9. Message hierarchy

When a page needs multiple messages, prioritize them in this order:

1. The user can move from an idea to a real result.
2. Product development becomes more accessible across disciplines.
3. Craft and iteration improve the quality of the work.
4. Collaboration helps teams build better products together.

Do not lead every message with collaboration. Choose the message that best matches the user's task and prove it with a specific capability.

## 10. Before-and-after examples

| Avoid | Prefer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Supercharge your workflow! | Move from review to ready for build, faster | Concrete benefit; no hype |
| Effortlessly collaborate in real time | Edit together and see changes as they happen | Shows the behavior |
| Something went wrong | We couldn't save this change. Try again. | Gives context and recovery |
| Utilize AI to magically generate content | Draft content with AI, then review and edit it | Plain language and user control |
| A revolutionary developer experience | See what's changed and what's ready to build | Specific and credible |
| Submit | Send invite | Matches the result |

## 11. Content review questions

Before shipping a string or flow, ask:

- Would a collaborative maker say this?
- Is the benefit about the user's work rather than our product's greatness?
- Can a reader picture what happens?
- Is every claim specific and supportable?
- Is the clever part still clear without explanation?
- Is technical language useful and understandable?
- Does the tone fit the stakes?
- Can any word, sentence, or punctuation mark be removed?
